A finding, and an interactive explainer

The seam between two islands is a maritime boundary

The pattern that appears where two coastlines' waterlines meet is the same geometric object UNCLOS calls a median line. That is not a metaphor. It is also, computed the way this renderer computes it, wrong by a knowable amount — and the amount has a closed form.

I One picture, two vocabularies

The same drawing, read twice

Drag either island. Then switch the labels: nothing in the drawing changes, only what the pieces of it are called.

A waterline is a level set of the distance to the coast. Where two coasts face each other, the level sets of one collide with the level sets of the other, and the collision locus is the medial axis of the water — an edge of the generalised Voronoi diagram of the two polygons.

UNCLOS builds the same object from the other end. Article 15 defines the median line as the line every point of which is equidistant from the nearest points on the two states' baselines. Equidistant from the nearest points is the definition of a Voronoi edge.

Kastrisios (2014) says so directly, in a footnote to his median-line construction: the median line segments are Voronoi edges, and the basepoints and straight baselines are the generators for which the plane is partitioned by nearest-neighbour rule. Kastrisios & Tsoulos (2016) built an automated delimitation methodology on exactly that.

Delaunay enters as the dual. A point equidistant from three basepoints is the circumcentre of a Delaunay triangle — and that is precisely a turning point of the boundary. Section IV builds one.

II The closer correspondence

A waterline is an outer limit

Everyone reaches for the median line first. But Article 4 is the tighter match: the outer limit of the territorial sea is a level set of the distance to the baseline, and nothing else.

Push the breadth slider up and the limit flattens: the farther it lies from the baseline, the less of the coast's sinuosity it reflects. The readout measures that directly, as limit length over straight-line length.

What does not change is the count of controlling basepoints — and that is worth knowing. On a polygonal baseline the controlling set is exactly the seaward-convex vertices, at every breadth, because a convex vertex always owns an arc of the limit however large the radius and a concave one never owns any. The familiar textbook figure showing fewer critical points at 4× the breadth is drawn from a smooth coast, where the controlling set is a continuum that thins out. Which model you are in changes the answer.

The waterline family of methodology.md §4.2 is this construction drawn at every radius simultaneously. Switch to 12 · 24 · 36 · 48 M and the figure becomes a four-line waterlines render — which is also a textbook diagram of the territorial sea, contiguous zone and beyond.

The replica line — tracé parallèle, the baseline copied and shifted seaward — is the method the ICJ described in the Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries case. Switch to it: the shaded band is the sea it gives away. A rigid copy cannot round a corner, so it runs landward of the true envelope at every turn — and because the shortfall is proportional to the breadth, raising the slider opens the band up. IHO C-51 disputes whether the replica line satisfies Article 4 from a normal baseline at all.

A third coincidence: the delimitation literature already contains a construction called the "water-line method", described as derived from an ancient manual cartographic technique for stepping a line off a coast. That is the same engraving practice this repository reimplements.

III What a boundary is made of

Three pieces, and only three

A strict equidistance line has exactly three kinds of segment, decided by what is nearest on each side. Drag the generators; the moving marker keeps both of its arms the same length.

Two basepoints give a straight perpendicular bisector. Two straight baselines give a straight angle bisector. A basepoint against a straight baseline gives a parabola — the basepoint is the focus, the baseline the directrix — and this is the case practitioners have to watch, because if you only take the endpoints of the straight baseline and skip the densification, you get a straight line where the law wants a curve, and quietly hand away sea.

Every one of these is a piece of the medial axis. The waterlines renderer produces all three for free, because it never constructs them: it measures distance and lets the collisions fall where they fall.

IV The construction, live

Turning points are circumcentres

Two coasts, their basepoints, and the strict equidistance line between them. Turn on the dual and every turning point grows the empty circle that proves it is one.

Each stretch of the line is controlled by one basepoint on each coast. Where control changes hands, the line kinks — and at that kink the point is equidistant from three basepoints at once, so a circle centred there through all three encloses no other basepoint. That empty-circumcircle test is the Delaunay condition, and it is exactly the check a delimitation expert performs to verify a candidate turning point.

Press Add one rock. A single feature, small enough that this repository's own level-of-detail pyramid would discard it, takes control of the middle of the boundary and moves it. The readout gives the sea it transfers.

This is why basepoint selection is a legal act and not a geometric one. Stage one of the ICJ's three-stage method (Black Sea, 2009) is choosing "the most appropriate base points"; courts have discarded them — Serpents' Island, Qit'at Jaradah — precisely because one rock should not carry a boundary.

Where this renderer fails. README: "Very small islands are dropped as you zoom out." In cartography that is a sensible simplification. Here it silently deletes the feature that decides the answer.

V The finding

Why this seam is not that boundary

Everything above is exact — in the plane. This renderer does not work in the plane. It works in Web Mercator pixels, and the difference is systematic, one-directional, and has a closed form.

Mercator is conformal, so the distance field is locally isotropic and the seam looks right anywhere you care to inspect it. But the scale factor k(φ) = sec φ varies with latitude, so a locus of equal pixel distance is not a locus of equal ground distance the moment the two coasts sit at different latitudes.

For two coasts running east–west at φm ± h, the Mercator ordinate y(φ) = ln tan(π/4 + φ/2) has y′ = sec φ and y″ = sec φ tan φ. The true median lies at the mid-latitude; the pixel median lies at the mid-ordinate. Expanding about φm:

Δφ  ≈  ½ tan(φm) h²    ⟹    Δs  ≈  ½ R tan(φm) h² φm the mid-latitude, h the half-separation in radians, R the Earth's radius

  • The bias is poleward, always. Δφ is positive in the north and symmetric in the south: the pixel median always sits nearer the higher-latitude coast. It is a systematic bias, not noise — it favours the same state every time you run it.
  • It vanishes at the equator and grows as tan φ. Which is exactly why the effect is invisible in the Nusantara renders that motivated this whole repository. Generalising from them would have been the mistake.
  • It is quadratic in the separation. Narrow straits are nearly safe. Wide opposite-coast delimitations are not.

VI What follows

Not a delimitation tool. Three real uses.

Delimitation needs coordinates on an ellipsoid; this produces a picture. methodology.md §4.1 is already explicit about it — "the result is a raster, not a vector; there is no path to export, hit-test, or label along" — and a maritime boundary is a coordinate list in a treaty annex. Closing the gap means replacing the metric, the output stage, the generator model and the level-of-detail pyramid: a different program that happens to share a theorem.

But the geometry is genuinely the same geometry, and three uses follow from that.

  • Sensitivity, at 60 fps. The standing complaint about strict equidistance is that it yields impractically many turning points needing simplification and negotiation. A distance field that redraws while you drag a candidate basepoint — section IV — shows which coastal features control which stretch, in the room, in real time. It feeds an exact computation. It does not replace one.
  • Teaching. Sections II and III are correct illustrations of the envelope of arcs and of the point–point, point–segment and segment–segment structure of a boundary. This page exists because that turned out to be the most useful thing in the finding.
  • Coarse to fine. Use the raster field to find candidate controlling basepoints cheaply, then hand only those few to exact geodesic computation.
