A finding

The seam between two islands is a maritime boundary

Where the waterlines of two coastlines meet, the line they make is the object UNCLOS calls a median line. This is not a metaphor. Drawn on a screen, the line is wrong by a known amount. The amount has a closed form.

Every figure is live. Move the coastlines with the mouse or touch.

I One picture, two vocabularies

The same drawing, read twice

Move either island. Then change the labels. Nothing in the drawing changes. Only the names of its parts change.

A waterline is a line of equal distance to the coast. Think of a wave that stops everywhere at the same distance from the shore: that line is a waterline. Where two coasts face each other, the waterlines of one meet the waterlines of the other. The line where they meet is the medial axis of the water. It is an edge of the generalised Voronoi diagram of the two polygons — the same "nearest point wins" rule, applied to whole coastlines.

UNCLOS builds the same object from the other end. Article 15 defines the median line. Every point of the median line is equidistant from the nearest points on the two states' baselines. "Equidistant from the nearest points" is the definition of a Voronoi edge.

Kastrisios (2014) states this in a footnote to his median-line construction. The median line segments are Voronoi edges. The basepoints and straight baselines are the generators. The plane is divided by the nearest-neighbour rule. Kastrisios & Tsoulos (2016) used this idea to build an automated delimitation method.

Delaunay enters as the dual. A point equidistant from three basepoints is the circumcentre of a Delaunay triangle. That point is also a turning point of the boundary. Section IV shows one.

II The closer match

A waterline is an outer limit

Most readers think of the median line first. Article 4 is the better match. The outer limit of the territorial sea is a line of equal distance to the baseline. It is nothing else.

Move the breadth slider up. The limit flattens. The farther the limit lies from the baseline, the less it shows the curves of the coast. The readout shows this as a number: the length of the limit divided by the straight distance across the figure.

Only the corners that stick out seaward matter. An arc is drawn from every point of the baseline, and the limit is the seawardmost of all those arcs. An arc from a seaward corner reaches the outside. An arc from a landward corner is always covered by another. So the controlling set is exactly the seaward-convex corners — and it does not change with the breadth.

The common textbook picture shows fewer controlling points at four times the breadth. That picture is drawn from a smooth coast, where the controlling set is a line that thins out. On a straight-lined baseline the corners never thin out. The model changes the answer.

This construction, drawn at every radius at once, is the family of waterlines. Switch to 12 · 24 · 36 · 48 M. The figure becomes a four-line waterlines picture. It is also the textbook picture of the territorial sea, the contiguous zone, and beyond.

The replica line is the baseline copied and moved seaward. In French it is the tracé parallèle. The ICJ described this method in the Anglo-Norwegian Fisheries case. Switch to it. The shaded band is the sea it gives away. A rigid copy cannot turn a corner. It runs landward of the true envelope at every corner. The shortfall is proportional to the breadth. Move the slider up and the band opens. IHO C-51 doubts whether the replica line satisfies Article 4 from a normal baseline at all.

There is a third match. The delimitation literature has a method called the "water-line method". It is described as a manual cartographic technique for stepping a line off a coast. That is the same technique shown here.

III What a boundary is made of

Three pieces, and only three

A strict equidistance line has exactly three kinds of segment. The kind depends on what is nearest on each side. Move the blue handles. The moving marker keeps both arms the same length.

Two basepoints give a straight line: the perpendicular bisector. Two straight baselines give a straight line: the angle bisector. A basepoint against a straight baseline gives a parabola. The basepoint is the focus. The baseline is the directrix. This is the case to watch. If you take only the two ends of the straight baseline, you draw a straight line where the law wants a curve. You quietly give away sea.

All three are pieces of the medial axis. The waterlines method makes all three without drawing any of them. It measures distance. The lines fall where the distances become equal.

Watch the readout as the marker moves. The two arms stay the same length: d(A) and d(B) match to a fraction of a pixel. That is the whole method, at one point at a time.

IV The construction, live

Turning points are circumcentres

Two coasts, their basepoints, and the strict equidistance line between them. Turn on the Delaunay dual. Each turning point grows the empty circle that proves it is a turning point.

One basepoint on each coast controls each stretch of the line. Where control changes hands, the line turns. At that turn, the point is equidistant from three basepoints at once. A circle centred there, through all three, encloses no other basepoint. An empty circle proves the turn: if another basepoint lay inside, that basepoint would be nearer and would have taken control. This empty-circle test is the Delaunay condition. It is also the check a delimitation expert makes to verify a candidate turning point.

Press Add one rock. One small feature takes control of the middle of the boundary and moves it. The readout shows how much sea it transfers.

This is why basepoint selection is a legal act, not a geometric one. The first stage of the ICJ's three-stage method (Black Sea, 2009) is to choose "the most appropriate base points". Courts have rejected basepoints. Two examples are Serpents' Island and Qit'at Jaradah. One rock should not carry a boundary.

Where the screen method fails. Very small islands disappear as you zoom out. In cartography this is a sensible simplification. On a screen it silently deletes the feature that decides the answer.

V The finding

Why this seam is not that boundary

Everything above is exact in the plane. A screen is not the plane. It is a Web Mercator map made of pixels. On that map, distance in pixels is not distance on the ground. The difference is systematic, one-directional, and it has a closed form.

Every figure on this page finds the seam the same way. It measures the distance from a point to each coast. It draws the point where the two distances are equal. Section V asks one question: distance in what units?

The law uses kilometres. A boundary point must be the same real-world distance from both baselines. That point is the true median, at the mid-latitude.

A screen has no kilometres. It has pixels. It draws the seam where the distance in pixels to each coast is equal. Call that the screen seam.

The two answers are not the same. On a Web Mercator map the scale changes with latitude: one degree of latitude is more pixels near the pole. When the two coasts sit at different latitudes, "equal pixels" and "equal kilometres" disagree.

Take two coasts running east–west at mid-latitude φm, with the coasts at φm ± h. The true median sits at the mid-latitude. The screen seam sits at the mid-ordinate: the vertical position halfway between the two coasts in pixels. Because one degree is more pixels near the pole, that halfway position is not the mid-latitude. It lies toward the higher-latitude coast. The shift is:

Δφ  ≈  ½ tan(φm) h²    ⟹    Δs  ≈  ½ R tan(φm) h² φm the mid-latitude, h the half-separation in radians, R the Earth's radius

The screen seam is not the result of a careless measurement. It is the correct answer to a different question.

  • The bias points poleward, always. Δφ is positive in the north and symmetric in the south. The screen seam always sits nearer the higher-latitude coast. The bias is systematic, not noise. It favours the same side every time.
  • It vanishes at the equator and grows as tan φ. At the equator the effect is zero. It grows as you move poleward. Tropical coastlines hide it. High-latitude coastlines do not.
  • It grows with the square of the separation. Narrow straits are nearly safe. Wide opposite-coast delimitations are not.
Δs for a 4° separation, computed from y(φ) exactly, not from the expansion. Published turning points are given to a fraction of an arc-second. That is about 3 m.

Raster resolution sets a second limit. A 500 km scene on a 1920 pixel screen gives about 260 m per pixel. The seam is known to ±130 m at best. The pixel-grid path adds its own error. At 10° this limit is the same size as the Mercator bias. Fixing one without the other buys nothing.

VI What follows

Not a delimitation tool. Three real uses.

Delimitation needs coordinates on an ellipsoid. The waterlines method makes a picture. The result is a raster, not a vector. There is no path to export, hit-test, or label along. A maritime boundary is a list of coordinates in a treaty annex. To close the gap, you would replace the metric, the output, the generators, and the control of detail. That is a different program that shares one theorem.

The geometry is the same geometry. Three uses follow.

  • Sensitivity, live. Strict equidistance gives too many turning points. Each one needs simplification and negotiation. A distance field that redraws while you move a candidate basepoint shows which coastal features control which stretch. It does this in real time. It feeds an exact computation. It does not replace one.
  • Teaching. Sections II and III are correct pictures of the envelope of arcs. They are also correct pictures of the point–point, point–segment, and segment–segment structure of a boundary. This page exists because that turned out to be the most useful part of the finding.
  • Coarse to fine. Use the raster field to find candidate controlling basepoints cheaply. Then give only those few to exact geodesic computation.
