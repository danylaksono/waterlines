An interactive explainer

Rendering waterlines at 60 fps

Old maps drew these ripples around every coastline. By hand. Here they are on a map you can drag, without dropping a single frame.

Every figure below is live — drag, scrub, toggle.

I What you're looking at

Not depth. Not data.

These are waterlines — also called water lining or coastal vignetting. They encode absolutely nothing about the real world.

Their spacing is purely aesthetic. They are a graphic device invented by nineteenth-century engravers to make land pop off the page.

That sounds like a footnote. It isn't: because the lines mean nothing in the world, they are measured in screen pixels rather than metres, and that single decision is what keeps the rendering cost bounded no matter how far you zoom out.

Credit. The rendering technique driving this entire project is Olivia Vane's, from her Observable notebook Drawing waterlines on maps. What's added here is the engineering to run it on a map that moves.

II The naive approach

The obvious way is a trap

Want a line 20 pixels out from the coast? Compute the coastline's offset curve at 20 pixels. Do it twelve times. Done.

Except offset curves self-intersect wherever the offset exceeds the local radius of curvature — and every bay on every coast is such a place. Drag the slider and watch the loops appear in the inlet. Each one has to be detected and cleaned up before you can draw anything.

That cleanup is the entire cost. Running turf.buffer twelve times over a real coastline takes about forty seconds. For one static image.

III The trick

Draw fat, erase thin

Stroking a path with a pen of width w paints exactly the set of points within w/2 of it. That's not a trick, it's the definition of a stroke.

So: draw a very fat line along the coast, then erase a slightly thinner one from its middle. What's left is an annular band at a known distance — a waterline, produced entirely by the rasteriser. Narrow the pen and repeat, and the rings nest inside each other.

No offset curve is ever constructed. The self-intersections from the last section are resolved implicitly, for free, by the rasteriser's fill rule.

Every pass is two strokes of the same path: one to paint, one to take the middle back out. The band that survives is the difference between two pen widths. 
// The entire rendering loop. Outermost pass first.
for (let i = 0; i <= count; i++) {
  const pen = penWidth(i);        // widest first, narrowing inward

  ctx.lineWidth = pen + lineWeight(i);
  ctx.stroke(coastline);        // a very fat line

  ctx.globalCompositeOperation = 'destination-out';
  ctx.lineWidth = pen;
  ctx.stroke(coastline);        // erase its middle
  ctx.globalCompositeOperation = 'source-over';
}

IV Constraints

Two rules you can't break

The technique is two strokes in a loop. Both of the things that can go wrong with it are things about the order and the shape of that loop.

  • Order is fixed. Each pass erases inside the previous one, so passes must run outward-in. A half-finished render is not a valid picture — which is why the engine renders off-screen and never shows you one.
  • Smooth the coast first. A wide pen amplifies every sharp vertex into a spike. Toggle to Raw and push the pen width up to watch it happen.

The smoothing is a centripetal Catmull–Rom spline (α = ½) — the only parameterisation in that family proven to produce no cusps and no self-intersections within a segment. Under a 90-pixel pen that guarantee is load-bearing, not cosmetic: a cusp becomes a spike forty-five pixels long.

The curve in every figure on this page is the real thing — the same centripetal Catmull–Rom to cubic-Bézier conversion as src/core/path.js, not a quadratic approximation.

V The one design rule

The setting everyone gets wrong

The lines are spaced by a power scale, which crowds them against the shore. So the tightest gap is always the innermost one — and it has a floor.

For N lines reaching E pixels out, inset s, with spacing exponent k, that innermost gap is roughly

Δmin  ≈  k(E − s) / N

Below about 2 px the ripples stop reading as lines and fuse into a dark halo. So there is a hard ceiling on how many lines you can ask for: N ≤ k(E − s) / 2.

Raise the count slider on its own and you'll cross it. This is not a hypothetical — an earlier version of this project shipped count: 22 at extent: 34, which violates the bound by a factor of three, and looked it.

The shipped antique preset sits at N = 14, E = 46 — a 2.0 px innermost gap, which is exactly on the bound rather than comfortably inside it.

VI An unearned gift

The accidental beauty

Drag the islands together. Where their ripples meet, a seam forms — and it is not a straight line, it bends according to the shape of both coasts.

That seam is the medial axis of the water becoming visible. It is precisely the locus where the offset curves of section II would have collided; between two islands it is an edge of their generalised Voronoi diagram.

The thing that makes the naive method hard is exactly what makes this one beautiful.

The dotted seam is computed, not drawn on: it is sampled from where the distance to one island equals the distance to the other. The ripples find it on their own — the two are independent.
That seam is also, exactly, the object UNCLOS calls a median line — and the ripple family is the envelope-of-arcs construction for the 12 and 24 M limits. Which is a longer story, told in the seam is a boundary, along with the reason this renderer must not be used to draw one.

VII Making it move

Never project per frame

A still image can afford one slow render. A map cannot — and the cost here is not the geometry, it's painted area.

Every pass paints a band twice the extent wide along every visible coast, twice over. Across an archipelago that is tens of millions of pixels per frame.

The structural escape: at pitch 0, Web Mercator to screen is exactly affine. So project the coastline into a Path2D once, and let a frame be six numbers handed to ctx.setTransform. JavaScript never touches a vertex again.

And the catch, which is a real one: a tilted view is projective, not affine, and a 2D canvas has no perspective divide. Pitch isn't unimplemented here — it's impossible.

The same limit holds on the GL path of section IX: its transform is a mat3 too, so it buys speed rather than perspective.

Two lessons that cost me real time

Median frame interval while dragging during a refresh.

  • You cannot time canvas drawing. A 2D canvas records commands and rasterises them later, so performance.now() around forty stroke() calls happily reported 0.1 ms for frames that were taking 100 ms. Forcing a flush with getImageData does make the number honest — and costs more than the drawing it was measuring. Pace on the observed interval between frames instead; it needs no readback at all.
  • Pacing isn't enough — you have to yield. One pass over a continental coastline costs about 130 ms, and one pass is the smallest unit that leaves the bitmap in a valid state. No step count fits 130 ms into a 16.6 ms frame. So while you are actually moving the map, the refresh stops dead and a cached bitmap is blitted instead; it resumes the moment you let go. That single change is the difference between the two bars above.

IX The new default

Every waterline at once

Everything above describes the canvas renderer: N wide strokes, each with its middle erased. It still ships, and it still runs everywhere. It is no longer what you get by default.

The identity, run backwards

Section III's stroke-and-erase pair keeps exactly the pixels whose distance to the coast falls between (w − t)/2 and w/2. Read that again with the emphasis moved: the N-pass loop is N slices of one function — D(x), the distance to the shore.

So if D is known per pixel, the slices need not be constructed at all. Waterline i sits at R(i) = E + (I − E)(i/n)e. Invert it:

ι(d)  =  n ((d − E) / (I − E))1/e

and every distance yields a fractional waterline index. Round to the nearest integer j, recompute R(j) exactly, shade the band around it. This is not an approximation of the canvas picture: it is the same R, evaluated at the same integers.

Antialiasing then arrives free, and better than before. ‖∇D‖ = 1 almost everywhere, so a half-pixel smoothstep on |d − R(j)| is exactly correct coverage — at any resolution, with no derivative estimation.

Animation is one subtraction

The canvas renderer animates by precomputing whole pictures and playing them back. Here the phase is a shader uniform: put the lines at j − p instead of j, cross-fade the two ends so the loop closes, and that is the entire mechanism. Scrub the phase slider above — the field underneath never changes. 

// The whole picture, once per pixel. N is a uniform, not a loop.
float d   = distance(texture(u_field, uv).xy, gl_FragCoord.xy);
float t   = clamp((d - u_extent) / (u_inset - u_extent), 0.0, 1.0);
float idx = pow(t, 1.0 / u_spacing) * u_n;   // fractional index

float j = floor(idx + u_phase + 0.5);      // the nearest real line
float r = radiusOf(j - u_phase);           // its exact radius

// |grad d| == 1, so half a pixel is exactly right.
float aa    = 0.5 / u_ratio;
float cover = 1.0 - smoothstep(halfW - aa, halfW + aa, abs(d - r));
Where D comes from. A screen-space jump flood (Rong & Tan, 2006): rasterise the coast with every covered pixel storing its own coordinates, then propagate the nearest known seed over a halving step. Nothing beyond the extent is ever read, so the flood starts at 2⌈log₂E⌉ rather than half the image — eight passes instead of eleven at E = 46 px. Sign comes from an even-odd stencil pass, the same rule as ctx.fill(path, 'evenodd'), so both renderers share an identical land mask.
What the figure really does. With two islands it is cheaper to compute D exactly, edge by edge, than to flood it — so it does, once, and reports how long that took. Everything after is the shading pass above, in JavaScript. The point is not the absolute numbers but which of the two moves when you drag a slider.

The ratio is not the finding. The slopes are. The canvas renderer is linear in N, exactly as the stroke-and-erase loop predicts. The distance field is flat — 1.27 to 1.45 ms across a sixteen-fold increase in line count, which is measurement noise. N has left the cost model.

Which is why the two lessons in the previous section stop applying. Both were about hiding the cost of re-rasterising N waterlines. Here there is nothing to hide, and six mechanisms built to hide it are simply gone.

The honest costs. WebGL2 with float render targets is required — seeds are coordinates, and 16-bit floats lose integer precision above 2048, which is inside an ordinary viewport at devicePixelRatio 2. Two RG32F viewport buffers are held permanently, about 10 MB each at 1440×900. A WebGL drawing buffer is discarded once composited, so export goes through an explicit snapshot(). And a true Euclidean field rounds convex corners and resolves concave ones on the medial axis, where the canvas path bevels a smoothed curve: the two pictures agree on 77% of sampled pixels, with total ink within 5%.
  • Raster cache a bitmap to show while a new one renders — gone, every frame is fresh
  • Visible-path cache per-frame cull and path assembly — gone, the level is one mesh
  • Step scheduling spread a refresh across frames — gone, nothing is long enough to split
  • Draft ladder coarse first, refine at rest — gone, there is nothing to wait for
  • Adaptive resolution trade sharpness under load — gone, nothing gives way
  • Animation frame cycle a precomputed loop of bitmaps — gone, the phase is a uniform
  • LOD pyramid amortise geometry work over zooms — kept, it was always about geometry
  • The canvas renderer coverage, a reference to difference against, and the double-blended overlaps that are part of the look — kept, on purpose

Selection is one option: renderer: 'auto' takes the distance field wherever the platform allows it, and falls back to the canvas where it does not. Both engines present the same interface, so the choice is invisible to callers.
