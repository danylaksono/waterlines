An interactive explainer

Rendering waterlines at 60 fps

Old maps drew these ripples around every coastline. By hand. Here they are on a map you can drag, without dropping a single frame.

Every figure below is live — drag, scrub, toggle.

I What you're looking at

Not depth. Not data.

These are waterlines — also called water lining or coastal vignetting. They encode absolutely nothing about the real world. Their spacing is purely aesthetic. They are a graphic device invented by nineteenth-century engravers to make land pop off the page.

That sounds like a footnote. It isn't: because the lines mean nothing in the world, they are measured in screen pixels rather than metres, and that single decision is what keeps the rendering cost bounded no matter how far you zoom out.

Credit. The rendering technique driving this entire project is Olivia Vane's, from her Observable notebook Drawing waterlines on maps. What's added here is the engineering to run it on a map that moves.

II The naive approach

The obvious way is a trap

Want a line 20 pixels out from the coast? Compute the coastline's offset curve at 20 pixels. Do it twelve times. Done.

Except offset curves self-intersect wherever the offset exceeds the local radius of curvature — and every bay on every coast is such a place. Drag the slider and watch the loops appear in the inlet. Each one has to be detected and cleaned up before you can draw anything.

That cleanup is the entire cost. Running turf.buffer twelve times over a real coastline takes about forty seconds. For one static image.

III The trick

Draw fat, erase thin

Stroking a path with a pen of width w paints exactly the set of points within w/2 of it. That's not a trick, it's the definition of a stroke.

So: draw a very fat line along the coast, then erase a slightly thinner one from its middle. What's left is an annular band at a known distance — a waterline, produced entirely by the rasteriser. Narrow the pen and repeat, and the rings nest inside each other.

No offset curve is ever constructed. The self-intersections from the last section are resolved implicitly, for free, by the rasteriser's fill rule. 

// The entire rendering loop. Outermost pass first.
for (let i = 0; i <= count; i++) {
  const pen = penWidth(i);        // widest first, narrowing inward

  ctx.lineWidth = pen + lineWeight(i);
  ctx.stroke(coastline);        // a very fat line

  ctx.globalCompositeOperation = 'destination-out';
  ctx.lineWidth = pen;
  ctx.stroke(coastline);        // erase its middle
  ctx.globalCompositeOperation = 'source-over';
}

IV Constraints

Two rules you can't break

  • Order is fixed. Each pass erases inside the previous one, so passes must run outward-in. A half-finished render is not a valid picture — which is why the engine renders off-screen and never shows you one.
  • Smooth the coast first. A wide pen amplifies every sharp vertex into a spike. Toggle to Raw and push the pen width up to watch it happen.

The smoothing is a centripetal Catmull–Rom spline (α = ½) — the only parameterisation in that family proven to produce no cusps and no self-intersections within a segment. Under a 90-pixel pen that guarantee is load-bearing, not cosmetic: a cusp becomes a spike forty-five pixels long.

The curve in every figure on this page is the real thing — the same centripetal Catmull–Rom to cubic-Bézier conversion as src/core/path.js, not a quadratic approximation.

V The one design rule

The setting everyone gets wrong

The lines are spaced by a power scale, which crowds them against the shore. That means the tightest gap is always the innermost one, and it is roughly

Δmin  ≈  k(E − s) / N

for N lines reaching E pixels out, inset s, with spacing exponent k. Below about 2 px the ripples stop reading as lines and fuse into a dark halo. So there is a hard ceiling on how many lines you can ask for: N ≤ k(E − s) / 2.

Raise the count slider on its own and you'll cross it. This is not a hypothetical — an earlier version of this project shipped count: 22 at extent: 34, which violates the bound by a factor of three, and looked it.

VI An unearned gift

The accidental beauty

Drag the islands together. Where their ripples meet, a seam forms — and it is not a straight line, it bends according to the shape of both coasts.

That seam is the medial axis of the water becoming visible. It is precisely the locus where the offset curves of section II would have collided; between two islands it is an edge of their generalised Voronoi diagram.

The thing that makes the naive method hard is exactly what makes this one beautiful.

The dotted seam is computed, not drawn on: it is sampled from where the distance to one island equals the distance to the other. The ripples find it on their own — the two are independent.

VII Making it move

Never project per frame

A still image can afford one slow render. A map cannot — and the cost here is not the geometry, it's painted area. Every pass paints a band twice the extent wide along every visible coast, twice over. Across an archipelago that is tens of millions of pixels per frame.

The structural escape: at pitch 0, Web Mercator to screen is exactly affine. So project the coastline into a Path2D once, and let a frame be six numbers handed to ctx.setTransform. JavaScript never touches a vertex again.

And the catch, which is a real one: a tilted view is projective, not affine, and a 2D canvas has no perspective divide. Pitch isn't unimplemented here — it's impossible.

VIII What it cost to learn

Two lessons that cost me real time

99.4 ms

16.6 ms
Yields to the gesture

Median frame interval while dragging during a refresh.

  • You cannot time canvas drawing. A 2D canvas records commands and rasterises them later, so performance.now() around forty stroke() calls happily reported 0.1 ms for frames that were taking 100 ms. Forcing a flush with getImageData does make the number honest — and costs more than the drawing it was measuring. Pace on the observed interval between frames instead; it needs no readback at all.
  • Pacing isn't enough — you have to yield. One pass over a continental coastline costs about 130 ms, and one pass is the smallest unit that leaves the bitmap in a valid state. No step count fits 130 ms into a 16.6 ms frame. So while you are actually moving the map, the refresh stops dead and a cached bitmap is blitted instead; it resumes the moment you let go. That single change is the difference between the two bars above.
